## Pitch 3: The Player Pitch (2 minutes)
You're Contractor #0447, working for Biosphere Management Group—a pharmaceutical mega-corp that definitely didn't cause the bug apocalypse and definitely isn't sending you into lethal situations with garbage equipment and plausible deniability.

**Hazard Pay** is a top-down pixel-art roguelike where you fight mutated insects the size of German Shepherds using "intentionally broken" exterminators. Each of the 5+ unlockable characters has one overpowered ability and one brutal constraint:

- **The Flamethrower** spreads fire infinitely between enemies—but standing still for even a second ignites fuel leaking beneath you
- **The Beekeeper** converts kills into ghost bee allies that scale your damage—but your HP pool IS your bee count (lose bees = lose HP)
- **The Cryo-Tech** shatters frozen enemies in chain reactions—but can't damage anything that isn't frozen
- **The Janitor** deals triple damage at max mop range—but enemies inside your guard take zero damage
- **The Sprayer** coats herself in damage-absorbing foam—but slows down proportionally to armor gained

Every run is 25 rooms with branching paths leading to shops, elite enemies, and "unknown" events. After each room, choose 1 of 3 upgrades across Power/Defense/Utility categories. Level up to auto-heal and unlock major path abilities at levels 10, 20, and 30—these are massive power spikes that define your build.

Enemies are designed as "teachers"—each type exists to force you to learn a mechanic:
- **Roaches** swarm in numbers (teaches AOE priority)
- **Spiders** drop from ceilings and lay web traps (teaches spatial awareness)
- **Wasps** fly and attack at range (teaches target prioritization)
- **Beetles** have armor that requires piercing damage (teaches build diversity)
- **Larvae** evolve into stronger forms if not killed quickly (teaches tempo and burst damage)

Beat the final boss to unlock Endless Mode—a separate infinite-scaling gauntlet where you keep leveling past 30 for permanent character-specific buffs. See how long you survive.

The story? It's there if you want it. Hidden documents scattered in secret rooms. Room names that hint at tragedy ("Former Daycare"). Bestiary entries with redacted sections. Upgrade descriptions that mention "field testing casualties." Achievement names like "What Happened to Gary?" Piece together the corporate conspiracy at your own pace—or ignore it entirely and just burn bugs.

**For fans of:** Enter the Gungeon's frantic combat, Vampire Survivors' power scaling, Risk of Rain's asymmetric characters, Hades' tight room-clearing action, and Inscryption's environmental mysteries.

## Pitch 5: The Design Philosophy Pitch (Developer-Focused)
**Hazard Pay** explores three interconnected design pillars:

**1. Intentionally Broken Characters (Asymmetric Mastery)**  
Most roguelikes give players versatile toolkits and test execution. We give players *broken* toolkits and test adaptation. Each character has one overpowered mechanic that would trivialize the game—balanced by a constraint so punishing it threatens to make them unplayable. The Flamethrower spreads fire infinitely, but fuel leaks create hazard zones forcing constant movement. The Beekeeper scales damage through summoned allies, but their HP pool IS their bee count—damage kills bees before touching base health.

This creates a "skill expression curve" where beginners struggle with the constraint while experts weaponize it. The Flamethrower's fuel leak becomes a zoning tool. The Beekeeper's shared HP becomes tactical ablative armor. Characters don't just feel different—they play by different rules.

**2. Enemies as Teachers (Systemic Tutorialization)**  
Every enemy type exists to teach a specific mechanic through repeated, escalating exposure:
- Roaches swarm in numbers, teaching AOE priority (kill many fast)
- Spiders web trap and ceiling drop, teaching spatial awareness (watch above/below)
- Wasps fly and kite, teaching target priority (flying threats first)
- Beetles armor up, teaching build diversity (need armor penetration)
- Larvae evolve if ignored, teaching tempo (burst damage windows)

This "enemies as curriculum" approach front-loads learning through gameplay, not tutorials. By room 10, players understand the language. By room 20, they're fluent. By Endless Mode, they're reading sentences the game never wrote.

**3. Environmental Storytelling (The Iceberg Model)**  
The core loop is pure action—no cutscenes, no dialogue, no forced narrative. But players who explore find fragments: collectible documents with contradictory dates, room names like "Former Daycare" and "Employee Housing Block C," bestiary entries with [REDACTED] sections, achievements titled "What Happened to Gary?"

The lore is an *option*, not an obligation. Speedrunners never see it. Completionists piece together a corporate conspiracy involving tainted agricultural compounds, regulatory capture, contractor disposability, and a mega-corp that profits from the apocalypse it caused. Forums debate theories. No one gets a definitive answer—because the mystery is the reward.

**Target Audience:**  
Players who value mechanical depth over power fantasy. Fans of asymmetric gameplay (Risk of Rain, Nuclear Throne), systemic interactions (Hades, Binding of Isaac), and subtle storytelling (Inscryption, Obra Dinn). Not for players seeking straightforward power scaling or explicit narratives.

**Development Focus:**  
Polish over content. Six perfectly balanced characters > twelve generic ones. Tight combat feel, responsive controls, readable visual clarity in chaos. Every enemy type pulls double duty as mechanic teacher and threat. Every upgrade creates build-defining synergies, not stat padding.

# 📜 The Lore in Three Paragraphs

## The Corporate Apocalypse

In 2023, pharmaceutical mega-corporation Biosphere Management Group (BMG) acquired a small agrochemical startup called Verdantix for $4.2 billion, gaining access to their revolutionary growth accelerant Compound V-7—a biochemical marvel that could end world hunger by tripling crop yields. Within weeks of acquisition, BMG gutted the safety team, replaced expensive stabilizer compounds with cheap alternatives, and rushed the reformulated "Verdant-X" to market. The original formula required 90 days of controlled cultivation; BMG's version shipped in 12. What the fired scientists tried to warn them was that V-7's growth acceleration hijacks mitochondrial function in *any* organism—and without proper stabilizers, the compound doesn't discriminate between plants and animals. When the tainted fertilizer hit the water table in 2024, ordinary insects exposed to environmental chemicals began mutating: roaches in grease-saturated kitchens developed incendiary glands, spiders in industrial zones secreted acidic webbing, wasps near power lines evolved bioelectric organs. The bugs didn't get magic powers—they just became biology's revenge for every environmental shortcut humanity ever took.

## The Cover-Up and Division 8

BMG's initial response was textbook corporate denial: blame climate change, silence whistleblowers, deploy experimental pesticides that only made survivors stronger. When a mutated roach queen breached a distribution center in August 2024 and killed 14 employees, they tried controlled incineration—which aerosolized the mutagenic compound and spread it to 17 additional counties. By November, coordinated infestations hit six major cities with 847 confirmed casualties, and BMG's board faced a choice: spend $340 billion to fix the problem and admit fault (destroying the company), or create a deniable extermination workforce and maintain plausible deniability. They chose the latter. Division 8 was chartered in December 2024, recruiting from the desperate, indebted, and legally compromised—people who'd sign liability waivers for minimum wage and a company van. The official cover story blames "invasive species from Southeast Asia," distributed through BMG-owned media outlets. Most citizens believe it because the alternative means admitting their salad was grown in apocalypse juice.

## Your Role as Contractor #0447

You are the latest disposable asset in BMG's extermination program, assigned a contractor number that starts at #0447—a detail that raises questions for anyone who wonders what happened to contractors #0001 through #0446. Your job is simple: clear infested sectors using second-hand equipment held together with duct tape and corporate liability waivers. Each deployment sends you into former homes, schools, and office buildings where the mundane turned horrific—wedding photos covered in webbing, children's backpacks abandoned in hive rooms, birthday cakes left uneaten. BMG provides the bare minimum: a work van that doubles as a mobile armory, upgrades labeled "warranty void" and "prototype only," and health pickups branded as "VitaJuice™" (the same soda in company vending machines). The death screen doesn't say "Game Over"—it says "CONTRACT TERMINATED. CONTRACTOR #XXXX UNRECOVERED." You're not a hero. You're a statistic with a flamethrower, cleaning up someone else's disaster for hazard pay while the stock price climbs 8% year-to-date. The truth is buried in redacted documents, fragmented memos, and room names that hint at tragedies the company PR team scrubbed from the news. But that's optional—you can ignore it all and just burn bugs. Most contractors do. Right up until they become "unrecovered."
